# Standard Alpha Testing / Early Product Feedback Survey Questions and Metrics (SaaS Creative Collaboration)

## What are the standard validated instruments used in alpha/usability testing (SUS, UMUX-Lite, NPS, CSAT, SEQ, CES, product-market fit Sean Ellis, etc.) and when to use each?

### Takeaway
Use **task-level** instruments (SEQ + completion) during scripted alpha tasks; use **short study-level** instruments (UMUX-Lite / UX-Lite, CSAT, intent-to-continue) for post-session/post-week surveys; reserve **SUS** for periodic overall usability benchmarks; use **NPS** for relationship/loyalty trends (not task diagnosis); use **CES** for specific service/support or “handle my issue” interactions; use **Sean Ellis PMF** only after users have experienced the core product enough times—not as a day-one alpha metric.

### Cited Findings
- MeasuringU recommends collecting both **task-level** metrics (completion, time, post-task ease) and **study-level** metrics (SUS, SUPR-Q, UX-Lite, satisfaction, NPS); task metrics are more diagnostic, while study-level metrics capture broader attitudes that tasks alone miss (~3–6% shared variance with completion/time/errors; ~41% with post-task ease). — [MeasuringU: Why Collect Task- and Study-Level Metrics?](https://measuringu.com/why-collect-task-and-study-metrics/)
- Study-level metrics are typically asked **once per study**; common constructs are overall satisfaction, perceived usability, perceived usefulness, and behavioral intentions (purchase/recommend/continue). — [MeasuringU: A Guide to Study-Based UX Metrics](https://measuringu.com/guide-to-study-based-ux-metrics/)
- **SUS (System Usability Scale, Brooke 1986)**: 10 items, 5-point agreement (1 = Strongly disagree … 5 = Strongly agree). Exact items: (1) “I think that I would like to use this system frequently.” (2) “I found the system unnecessarily complex.” (3) “I thought the system was easy to use.” (4) “I think that I would need the support of a technical person to be able to use this system.” (5) “I found the various functions in this system were well integrated.” (6) “I thought there was too much inconsistency in this system.” (7) “I would imagine that most people would learn to use this system very quickly.” (8) “I found the system very cumbersome to use.” (9) “I felt very confident using the system.” (10) “I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with this system.” Scoring: odd items = response−1; even items = 5−response; sum × 2.5 → 0–100. Average across ~500 studies ≈ **68**; ≥80.3 ≈ “A” / top ~10%. Reliable even at very small n, but confidence intervals are wide; **not diagnostic** of what to fix. — [MeasuringU: Measuring Usability with the SUS](https://measuringu.com/sus/); [Lewis & Sauro HCII 2009 PDF](https://measuringu.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/Lewis_Sauro_HCII2009.pdf)
- **When to use SUS**: after a usability/alpha session or periodically for whole-product benchmarking of software/hardware interfaces; industry-standard reference databases exist. — [MeasuringU: 15 Metrics for UX Benchmarking](https://measuringu.com/ux-benchmark-metrics/); [Great Question UX survey guide](https://www.greatquestion.com/ux-research/ux-survey-questions)
- **UMUX**: 4 items, 7-point scales (2 positive / 2 negative), intended as shorter SUS analog aligned to ISO 9241 usability. Items include: “[This system’s] capabilities meet my requirements.”; “Using [this system] is a frustrating experience.”; “[This system] is easy to use.”; “I have to spend too much time correcting things with [this system].” — [MeasuringU: From the SUS to the UMUX-Lite](https://measuringu.com/umux-lite/)
- **UMUX-Lite** (Lewis et al.): 2 all-positive items: “[This system’s] capabilities meet my requirements.” and “[This system] is easy to use.” on 5- or 7-point agree/disagree. Reliability reported α ≈ .82–.86; correlates with SUS ≈ .81–.83 and LTR/NPS ≈ .72–.74. Regression to estimate SUS (7-point form cited by MeasuringU): `SUS = 0.65 × ((Item1 + Item2 − 2) × (100/12)) + 22.9`. Use when space is tight (in-product, end of another survey, many internal products). Alternate usefulness wordings with comparable means include “{Product}’s features meet my needs” and “{Product} does what I need it to do.” — [MeasuringU: UMUX-Lite](https://measuringu.com/umux-lite/); [ACM CHI UMUX-LITE abstract](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2470654.2481287); [MeasuringU: UMUX-Lite Usefulness Alternates](https://measuringu.com/umux-lite-useful-alternative/)
- **UX-Lite**: MeasuringU’s simplified UMUX-Lite variant; ease item same as UMUX-Lite; usefulness item uses simpler wording “{system}’s features meet my needs”; **5-point** scales for easier integration with SUS/SUPR-Q. — [MeasuringU: Guide to Study-Based UX Metrics](https://measuringu.com/guide-to-study-based-ux-metrics/)
- **SEQ (Single Ease Question)**: ask **immediately after each task**. Common wording: “Overall, how difficult or easy was the task to complete?” (also used: “How easy or difficult was it to complete this task?”). **7-point** semantic differential, endpoints Very Difficult (1) … Very Easy (7); typically number points and label ends only. Historical mean ≈ **5.5** (≈5.3–5.6). Correlation with completion/time ≈ **r = .5**. Recommended open follow-up when score **< 5**: briefly describe why difficult. — [MeasuringU: 10 Things To Know About the SEQ](https://measuringu.com/seq10/); [MeasuringU: Evolution of the SEQ](https://measuringu.com/evolution-of-seq/); [MeasuringU: Adjective Interpretations of SEQ](https://measuringu.com/adjective-interpretations-of-seq-scores/)
- **NPS / Likelihood to Recommend**: single item, **0–10**: how likely to recommend the experience/brand/product; NPS = % promoters (9–10) − % detractors (0–6). Ubiquitous loyalty/word-of-mouth metric; MeasuringU advises **not** relying on NPS alone for interface measurement—pair with SEQ/completion and SUS/UMUX-Lite/SUPR-Q. — [MeasuringU: Guide to Study-Based UX Metrics](https://measuringu.com/guide-to-study-based-ux-metrics/); [MeasuringU: CE11 and NPS](https://measuringu.com/ce11-nps/); [Great Question](https://www.greatquestion.com/ux-research/ux-survey-questions)
- **CSAT / General Satisfaction**: single broad item rating satisfaction with brand/product/experience (commonly 5- or 7-point satisfied scales, or branded variants). Microsoft **NSAT** uses a **4-point** satisfaction item scored as % Very Satisfied − % of two dissatisfied boxes. Attribute-level satisfaction (feature/page/support) is more diagnostic than overall CSAT alone. — [MeasuringU: Guide to Study-Based UX Metrics](https://measuringu.com/guide-to-study-based-ux-metrics/); [MeasuringU: 15 Metrics for UX Benchmarking](https://measuringu.com/ux-benchmark-metrics/)
- **CES (Customer Effort Score)**: originally (HBR/CEB) effort-focused; **CES 2.0 / Gartner-style** agreement statement: “To what extent do you agree or disagree with the following statement: The company made it easy for me to handle my issue.” on **1–7** (Strongly disagree … Strongly agree). One common scoring: % of responses 5–7 (top-3-box). Best for **post-interaction / support / issue-handling**, not as a substitute for whole-product usability; often paired with CSAT + NPS. Product adaptations exist (e.g., “{Product} made it easy for me to get started”) but then treat as local benchmarks, not industry CES norms. — [Questionstar CES guide](https://questionstar.com/blog/customer-effort-score-ces/); [Giva CES overview](https://www.givainc.com/blog/customer-effort-score/); [Salesforce/AppExchange CES note](https://appexchange.salesforce.com/partners/servlet/servlet.FileDownload?file=00P3A00000nkp79UAA)
- **Sean Ellis Product-Market Fit (PMF) survey**: exact core question: **“How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?”** Options: Very disappointed / Somewhat disappointed / Not disappointed (it isn’t really that useful) / N/A – I no longer use [product]. Score = **% Very disappointed**; Ellis benchmark often cited: **≥40%** associated with products that found strong growth (vs. those that struggled below ~40%). Survey the right users: experienced core offering, used **≥2 times**, used in **last two weeks**. Do not alter wording if comparing to the 40% benchmark. Common follow-ups (Superhuman/Ellis-style): main benefit; who would benefit most; how to improve; (VentureHacks/survey.io also includes discovery, alternatives, recommendation). — [Learning Loop PMF guide](https://learningloop.io/plays/product-market-fit-survey); [Real Startup Book PMF](https://anteo.gitbooks.io/the-real-startup-book/content/product-market_fit_survey/description.html); [Venture Hacks / survey.io](https://venturehacks.com/measure-fit); [MeasuringU study metrics (PMF as top-box)](https://measuringu.com/guide-to-study-based-ux-metrics/)
- Startup/alpha practice often mixes: short contextual in-product ratings (1–5 experience of a workflow), failure-mode MCQ, optional bug detail, comparison to prior workflow, follow-up consent (Userpilot); or ~12-item ~5-minute beta surveys including ease of main task, PMF disappointment, NPS, device/browser (betatest.ing). Maze beta template emphasizes overall experience opinion scale + challenges open text. — [Userpilot beta feedback forms](https://userpilot.com/blog/beta-testing-feedback-form-template-best-practices-and-examples/); [betatest.ing survey questions](https://betatest.ing/beta-testing/beta-feedback-survey-questions); [Maze Beta Testing Template](https://maze.co/templates/beta-testing-feedback/)

### Inferences
- For an early SaaS creative collaboration **alpha**, a practical quantitative stack is: **SEQ after core tasks** + **UMUX-Lite (or UX-Lite) once per session/week** + **feature satisfaction matrix** + binary/usage filters; add **SUS** only if you need an external benchmark comparable to published SUS norms; add **Ellis PMF** after sustained use (not first login); use **NPS** sparingly as a trend line with an open “why” follow-up.
- Prefer validated wording over inventing new scales when you want comparable statistics; invent only for product-specific diagnostics after the standardized score.

### Gaps
- No single authoritative “alpha survey ISO standard” for SaaS creative tools was found; industry practice is a composite of UX psychometrics + startup PMF/beta templates.
- Primary Gartner CES 2.0 methodology paper was not fetched behind paywall; wording/scoring above relies on secondary industry summaries that consistently quote the same statement and 7-point agreement scale.
- Exact original Brooke 1996 PDF text was only partially available via secondary mirrors; SUS item list and scoring above match MeasuringU and Lewis & Sauro reproductions.

## What Likert scales and response formats are recommended for statistical aggregation?

### Takeaway
Use **odd-numbered bipolar scales with a neutral** for most UX items; prefer **5-point fully labeled Likert** for multi-item instruments (SUS, UX-Lite) and **7-point** (often end-labeled) for **single-item** measures (SEQ, CES 2.0) where extra discrimination matters; aggregate multi-item instruments with their published scoring (means or SUS-style transforms), report means with confidence intervals, and keep scale formats consistent within a study so feature scores are comparable.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g: **Likert** = agreement with statements (typically **5**, sometimes 7/9), usually **fully word-labeled**; a single question is a Likert **item**, not a full Likert **scale**. **Semantic differential** = bipolar adjectives (typically **7**), often end-labeled only (SEQ is an example). Tips: prefer **standardized** usability questionnaires when measuring ease; use conventional agreement labels; ensure true antonyms for semantic differentials; add optional “why” text; add **Not applicable** when relevance varies. — [NN/g: Rating Scales in UX Research](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/rating-scales/) (last reviewed Aug 7, 2026)
- MeasuringU on 5 vs 7 points: **7-point scales are slightly better** than 5 for discrimination, with diminishing returns after ~11 points; for **single-item** measures, more points help more (7/9/11 vs 5); for **multi-item** composites, fewer points can still work because items combine. Minimum for useful single-item UX measures is generally **≥5** options including direction, magnitude, and usually a neutral. — [MeasuringU: Should You Use 5 Or 7 Point Scales?](https://measuringu.com/scale-points/); [MeasuringU: Convert Between Five- and Seven-Point Scales](https://measuringu.com/convert-point-scales/)
- Linear conversion between 5- and 7-point ratings is possible for comparison: `x7 = (x5 − 1)(6/4) + 1`. — [MeasuringU: Convert Between Five- and Seven-Point Scales](https://measuringu.com/convert-point-scales/)
- For small and large samples, MeasuringU recommends reporting the **mean** for rating scales (often more useful than median); organizations also commonly report top-box / top-two-box depending on sensitivity needs. — [MeasuringU: Small Sample Sizes](https://measuringu.com/small-n/)
- SUS uses **5-point** Strongly disagree–Strongly agree; SEQ uses **7-point** Very Difficult–Very Easy; UMUX/UMUX-Lite originally **7-point**, with MeasuringU also collecting **5-point** UMUX-Lite/UX-Lite for SUS alignment; CES 2.0 commonly **7-point** fully labeled agreement; NPS uses **0–10** (11 points). — [MeasuringU SUS](https://measuringu.com/sus/); [MeasuringU SEQ](https://measuringu.com/seq10/); [MeasuringU UMUX-Lite](https://measuringu.com/umux-lite/); [Questionstar CES](https://questionstar.com/blog/customer-effort-score-ces/)
- NN/g notes alternating positive/negative SUS items can reduce acquiescence but also confuse respondents/analysts (Sauro & Lewis 2011); UMUX-Lite’s **all-positive** design avoids that coding pitfall for short surveys. — [NN/g Rating Scales](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/rating-scales/); [MeasuringU UMUX-Lite](https://measuringu.com/umux-lite/)
- Contrarian methods critique (tourism survey methods preprint): bipolar 5/7 formats can have low test-retest reliability and response-style bias; argues against treating them as an unexamined default. This conflicts with mainstream UX practice that still standardizes on 5/7 Likert and semantic differentials for SUS/SEQ/etc. — [Dolnicar OSF preprint](https://doi.org/10.31235/osf.io/8p4yc); contradicted in practice by [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/rating-scales/) and [MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/scale-points/)

### Inferences
- For Ensemble-style alpha stats: stick to **one primary scale family per construct**—e.g., SEQ 1–7 after tasks, UMUX-Lite/UX-Lite 1–5 (or 1–7) for product, identical 1–5 satisfaction for every feature row—so you can compare means/CI without rescaling gymnastics.
- Prefer published instrument scoring over ad-hoc “average of random Likert items.”

### Gaps
- No universal rule in sources that ordinal Likert data “must not” use means; UX practice commonly uses means + t-based CIs while acknowledging the scale-type debate.
- Few 2024–2026 primary psychometric revalidations of CES/NPS for creative SaaS specifically were found.

## What demographic / usage screening questions are standard for alpha cohorts?

### Takeaway
Keep screeners to roughly **3–7** multiple-choice questions focused on **ICP fit, problem frequency, current tools, device/environment, and feedback commitment**; collect demographics mainly for **segmentation**, and for PMF-style surveys additionally require recent, repeated core-product use.

### Cited Findings
- BetaTesting recommends limiting screening surveys to **most important** criteria (~**3–6** questions); too many filters shrink the pool. Common types: lifestyle, participation consent, technical/device, product/brand experience. — [BetaTesting: Screening Surveys](https://help.betatesting.com/en/articles/3915663-screening-surveys-screening-participants-for-beta-testing-user-research)
- Startup beta recruitment guidance proposes a **7-question starter screener**: (1) role and company size; (2) how they currently solve the problem; (3) tools used now; (4) device/OS/stack; (5) experience level with similar products (tag power vs typical users); (6) time they can give; (7) open text “Why does this problem matter to you?” plus an attention check. Separate **demographics** (B2B: role, company size, geography, industry) from **behavior** and **technographics**. — [Launching Next: Find Beta Testers](https://www.launchingnext.com/blog/find-beta-testers/)
- User research recruitment screeners commonly ask: current role/title; company size (B2B); tools used for [task]; frequency of relevant behavior; last time they did X; competitor employment exclusions. Behavioral criteria examples: “Uses project management software 3+ times per week.” — [CleverX: Recruit participants](https://cleverx.com/blog/how-to-recruit-participants-for-product-research/)
- Userpilot beta stages include **segmentation** questions such as: “Which of these best describes your role?”; “Which device and operating system will you test on?”; “How do you currently handle [the job your product does]?” — [Userpilot beta templates](https://userpilot.com/blog/beta-testing-feedback-form-template-best-practices-and-examples/)
- Sean Ellis / Real Startup Book: send PMF question to users who have **experienced the core offering**, used the product **at least twice**, and used it in the **last two weeks**; optionally pre-segment. — [Real Startup Book PMF](https://anteo.gitbooks.io/the-real-startup-book/content/product-market_fit_survey/description.html)
- MeasuringU tech-savviness options useful as alpha covariates: skill checklists (e.g., install router / set up phone); self-rating “I am tech savvy”; Pew Digital Savviness Classifier (internet frequency + confidence). — [MeasuringU: Guide to Study-Based UX Metrics](https://measuringu.com/guide-to-study-based-ux-metrics/)
- Usage intensity in early-access templates is often asked as ordered categories (e.g., Daily / A few times a week / Once or twice / Not yet). — [SurveyAnalytica Beta & Early Access template](https://surveyanalytica.com/en/templates/beta-early-access-feedback-survey/survey-beta-early-access-feedback-survey)

### Inferences
- For a creative collaboration SaaS alpha, prioritize: **role** (creator/editor/producer/admin), **team size**, **current collab tools**, **frequency of collaborative creative work**, **device/browser**, **willingness to complete feedback**, and **power-user vs typical** tag—then analyze scores by segment rather than over-filtering.
- Treat screening answers as both eligibility gates and later **covariates** for SUS/UMUX/SEQ comparisons.

### Gaps
- No single “standard demographic battery” unique to creative-collaboration platforms was found; sources give general B2B/SaaS patterns.
- Productboard-specific alpha screener templates were not located in search results within this pass.

## What bug-reporting and severity questions are commonly included?

### Takeaway
Alpha surveys typically capture **whether** something broke, **what was expected vs happened**, **context** (device/browser), and sometimes a **failure-mode category**; severity is more often tagged by the team (blocks core task vs minor) than self-rated by users on a validated psychometrics scale—pair survey flags with a dedicated bug form for reproduction detail.

### Cited Findings
- Userpilot in-test template quantitative + structured bug path: (1) “How was [workflow] just now?” **1–5 required**; (2) “What were you trying to accomplish…?” short text; (3) “Did anything almost stop you from finishing?” MCQ: confusing step / bug / slow performance / nothing; (4) optional “If you hit a bug, what did you expect to happen, and what happened instead?”; (5) comparison to prior job; (6) follow-up consent. — [Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/beta-testing-feedback-form-template-best-practices-and-examples/)
- betatest.ing bugs/quality block: “Did anything not work as expected? What were you doing?”; “Which device and browser did you mostly use?”; “Did anything feel slow?” Notes that survey bugs lack detail—point people to a **bug form** and follow up on serious items. Copy-ready survey includes those items plus main-task ease 1–5 and NPS. — [betatest.ing](https://betatest.ing/beta-testing/beta-feedback-survey-questions)
- Zonka 2026 beta guide performance/stability mid-beta closed questions (mostly yes/no or presence): errors/crashes; delays/lags; loading/refresh issues; performance on specific tasks; excessive resource use; OS/software compatibility. Recommends routing engineering via Jira/Slack. — [ZonkaFeedback beta testing survey](https://www.zonkafeedback.com/blog/beta-testing-survey)
- Klaritea beta ops guidance: tag reports by **severity** (blocks core task vs minor annoyance) and **frequency** (one tester vs pattern); prioritize high-severity × high-frequency; track weekly completion rate, actionable reports, recurring UX blockers. — [Klaritea beta recruitment](https://blog.klaritea.app/blog/beta-tester-recruitment)
- Maze beta template focuses on overall feature experience opinion scale + open challenges/additional feedback rather than a formal severity rubric. — [Maze Beta Testing Template](https://maze.co/templates/beta-testing-feedback/)

### Inferences
- Quantify bugs with **binary encounter rate** (% who hit a bug), **categorical failure mode**, and **optional severity self-tag** (e.g., Blocker / Major / Minor / Cosmetic) for triage—but treat self-severity as soft signal; validate with repro steps.
- Separate **quality survey** (stability/performance rates) from **usability instruments** so a crash-heavy build does not get misread solely as “hard to use.”

### Gaps
- No widely validated “bug severity Likert instrument” analogous to SUS was found; industry uses operational severity tags, not psychometric scales.
- Google Material-specific beta bug questionnaire guidance was not located in this research pass.

## What is a practical survey length for alpha testers (question count / time)?

### Takeaway
Aim for **~5 minutes** for an emailed/post-session alpha survey (~**8–12** closed questions plus 1–2 opens), keep **in-product micro-surveys to 1–3 items**, and prefer **multiple short surveys** over one long instrument; MeasuringU’s survey median is ~5 minutes, and length is the strongest abandonment predictor.

### Cited Findings
- MeasuringU analysis of 260 online studies: overall median **9 minutes**; surveys alone median **5 minutes** (IQR ~3–9); unmoderated usability studies median **14 minutes**. Prefers multiple short studies to reduce dropout/fatigue. — [MeasuringU: How Long Is the Typical Online Study?](https://measuringu.com/online-study-time/)
- MeasuringU survey design training: **best predictor of abandonment is length**; median MeasuringU survey ~**5 minutes**; shortest for intercepts, longer OK for email; use progress bar; pilot/soft launch. — [MeasuringU Survey Design PDF](https://measuringu.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/SurveyDesign_Hulu2020.pdf)
- SurveyMonkey analysis: respondents spend ~**5 minutes** on a **10-question** survey on average; abandon rates rise for surveys taking more than ~**7–8 minutes**; keep under **~10 minutes** for high engagement; time-per-question falls as surveys lengthen (satisficing risk). — [SurveyMonkey: How Long Should A Survey Be?](https://www.surveymonkey.com/curiosity/survey_completion_times/)
- Secondary citation of Hotjar (2024): on-site single-question surveys ~**40–60%** completion; **≥3 questions** can drop below **~15%** on-site; email post-experience surveys can run **5–7** questions. (Reported via CRO summary citing Hotjar—treat completion % as secondary.) — [Lionelz CRO Surveys](https://lionelz.com/en/blog/surveys-for-cro/)
- Userpilot: contextual **6-question** in-app beta form after key workflow. betatest.ing: “about **5 minutes**” copy-ready beta survey (~12 items mixing closed + open). Usertick feature satisfaction template claims ~**85 seconds** for a 3-question feature matrix pack. — [Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/beta-testing-feedback-form-template-best-practices-and-examples/); [betatest.ing](https://betatest.ing/beta-testing/beta-feedback-survey-questions); [Usertick feature satisfaction](https://usertick.com/en/templates/feature-satisfaction)
- Question brevity guidance: keep individual questions short (literature cited by MeasuringU includes suggestions like <20–25 words). — [MeasuringU: Clearer Survey Questions](https://measuringu.com/clearer-questions/)

### Inferences
- Structure alpha feedback as **layers**: (A) 1-item in-product pulse after critical actions; (B) 5-minute weekly alpha survey with UMUX-Lite + feature matrix + bug flag; (C) occasional longer task study with SEQ per task + SUS/UMUX at end.
- If including full **10-item SUS**, budget most of a short survey for it—or swap to **UMUX-Lite** for alpha to leave room for feature/bug items.

### Gaps
- Exact Hotjar primary 2024 completion-rate page was not fetched; figures above are via a secondary citation.
- No Ensemble-specific completion norms yet (product not in sources).

## How should multi-feature products structure surveys so feature scores can be compared?

### Takeaway
Rate features in a **matrix with one shared scale**, include a **Not used / N/A** column, keep **5–7 feature rows** per grid, collect **usage frequency** separately, then compare feature means (or top-box) and optionally cross usage × satisfaction into priority quadrants—never mix different scale lengths across features you want to compare.

### Cited Findings
- MeasuringU: general satisfaction is weakly diagnostic; ask satisfaction with **specific features/parts** of the experience for actionable attribute measures. — [MeasuringU: 15 Metrics for UX Benchmarking](https://measuringu.com/ux-benchmark-metrics/)
- Maze Matrix block: rows = statements/features, columns = shared response options; designed for feature satisfaction and Likert batteries; recommends **5–7 rows** to limit fatigue (max 10), **2–7** columns, consistent scale direction; results support comparing ratings across rows. — [Maze Matrix block](https://help.maze.co/articles/1713486615-collect-multi-dimensional-feedback-with-the-matrix-block)
- Feature importance/satisfaction templates: list features as rows with identical columns (e.g., Not used / Low / Medium / High / Essential, or Very dissatisfied → Very satisfied); include **Not used** so unused features are not falsely rated important/satisfying; segment by role/tenure. — [OneCraft product feedback example](https://onecraft.app/examples/forms/product-feedback-survey/); [SurveyMethods matrix guidance](https://surveymethods.com/kb/matrix-rating-scale/)
- Usertick: rate multiple product parts on the **same 5-point satisfaction scale**, then optionally allocate **100 points** across improvement priorities; interpret low satisfaction + high points as top fix. — [Usertick Feature Satisfaction](https://usertick.com/en/templates/feature-satisfaction)
- Formware “feature report card”: identical scale per module; skip if unused (non-usage is itself a signal); optional open “what would you improve about [module]?”; overall CSAT as anchor; analyze **usage × satisfaction** quadrants (beloved/used, used/unloved, loved/unused, unloved/unused). — [Formware Software Satisfaction / Feature Report Card](https://formware.io/templates/software-customer-satisfaction-survey-template/)
- Test Science notes UMUX-Lite can be administered for **components** of a system under test (multiple short scales), not only the whole product. — [Test Science: Measuring Usability](https://testscience.org/measuring-usability/)
- Task-based alternative for multi-feature products: SEQ after each feature-critical task enables **task-to-task comparison** of ease on the same 7-point metric (avg ~5.5). — [MeasuringU SEQ](https://measuringu.com/seq10/)

### Inferences
- For creative collaboration suites, define a stable **feature dictionary** (e.g., projects, realtime collab, comments, versions, sharing, media review) and reuse the same matrix every pulse so week-over-week feature means are comparable.
- Combine: **usage checkbox/frequency** + **shared satisfaction scale** + optional **100-point allocation** or importance rating; report per-feature mean, % N/A, and CI.

### Gaps
- Sources disagree slightly on maximum matrix size (Maze allows 10 rows but recommends 5–7); no hard psychometric maximum for feature count was found.
- Importance–performance formal IPA methodology papers were not deeply reviewed in this pass.

## What sample size / analysis tips apply to small alpha cohorts?

### Takeaway
Small alpha ns (**~5–30**) can still produce useful statistics if you report **confidence intervals**, expect to detect only **large** effects, prefer exploratory confidence levels (~**80–90%**) for early decisions, use appropriate estimators for completion rates/times, and segment cautiously—do not claim precise population benchmarks from a handful of testers.

### Cited Findings
- MeasuringU defines “small” usability samples as typically **5–30** users; statistics are valid but limited to detecting large differences (“binoculars, not telescopes”). — [MeasuringU: Best Practices for Small Sample Sizes](https://measuringu.com/small-n/)
- Confidence intervals: for continuous ratings use **t-based** CIs; for task times use **log-transform** then back-transform; for binary completion/yes-no use **Adjusted Wald**. CI widths of **~20–30 percentage points** are common at small n; bounds still useful (example: 6/8 failures ⇒ at least ~40% of population likely fail). — [MeasuringU: Small n](https://measuringu.com/small-n/); [Sauro & Lewis Adjusted Wald HFES paper](https://measuringu.com/papers/sauro-lewisHFES.pdf)
- Comparing two groups at small n: continuous → **two-sample t-test**; binary → **N−1 two-proportion** (or Fisher Exact when expected counts <1). — [MeasuringU: Small n](https://measuringu.com/small-n/)
- Point estimates: for small-sample completion rates, LaPlace / adjusted estimators often preferable to raw 100% with n=5; for rating scales report **means**; for times with n≲25 prefer **geometric mean** over median/arithmetic mean. — [MeasuringU: Small n](https://measuringu.com/small-n/)
- Confidence level choice by stakes: exploratory prototypes/early sentiment often **80%+**; industrial analyses often **90%+**; publication **95%+**. Lower confidence widens Type I risk but is sometimes appropriate when costs of delay exceed costs of being wrong. — [MeasuringU: How Confident Do You Need to Be?](https://measuringu.com/confidence-levels/)
- SUS remains reliable at very small samples (even n=2 mentioned), but population estimates are imprecise—always report a **CI around mean SUS**. — [MeasuringU: SUS](https://measuringu.com/sus/)
- SEQ: individual response styles vary; averages relative to database (~5.5) are more meaningful than over-interpreting single users; ~14% of failed tasks still rated “extremely easy.” — [MeasuringU: SEQ10](https://measuringu.com/seq10/)
- Sean Ellis PMF % Very Disappointed is a **top-box proportion**; with small alpha n the percentage is noisy—use Adjusted Wald-style intervals and restrict to qualified recent users before comparing to the **40%** rule of thumb. — [Learning Loop PMF](https://learningloop.io/plays/product-market-fit-survey); interval method from [MeasuringU Small n](https://measuringu.com/small-n/)

### Inferences
- Report alpha dashboards as: mean ± CI for UMUX-Lite/SUS/SEQ/feature ratings; % ± CI for bug encounter, task success, PMF very-disappointed; highlight only **large** gaps between features/tasks.
- Avoid declaring “we beat industry NPS/SUS” from n<30 without intervals; use scores primarily for **within-product** tracking (build-over-build, feature-vs-feature).

### Gaps
- No consensus minimum n specifically for “alpha SaaS survey validity” beyond general UX small-n guidance.
- Industry PMF 40% threshold is a **heuristic from Ellis’s startup benchmarking narrative**, not a peer-reviewed universal constant; treat with intervals and cohort-quality caveats.
